How to count calories from a photo

A photo of your meal gives you a quick estimate of its calories, protein, carbs and fat. Here's how to take a photo that works, and how to check the result so your log stays close to what you actually ate.

English Italiano Deutsch Türkçe

What a photo can and can't tell

A calorie counter that works from photos uses an AI model to recognize each food on the plate and estimate its portion. It then works out calories and macros from typical nutrition values for that food.

It works best with foods you can see clearly. It can't see what's hidden: the oil in the pan, the butter in a sauce, the sugar in a drink or the filling of a wrap. So treat the result as an estimate to check, not a measurement.

Five steps

  1. Get the whole plate in the frame

    Shoot from above or at a slight angle, in good light, with every item visible and nothing covering anything else. One plate per photo.

  2. Take the photo before you eat

    A full plate shows the real portion. If you forgot, log the meal by search instead.

  3. Check every item

    Fix a name that's wrong, adjust a portion that looks too big or too small, and remove anything you didn't eat.

  4. Add what the camera can't see

    Cooking oil, butter, dressings, sauces, and sugar or milk in drinks add calories without showing up in a photo. Add them as separate items.

  5. Save it to the right meal

    Add it to breakfast, lunch, dinner or a snack on the right day, so your daily total adds up.

Counting calories from a photo in Ringolo

  1. Open Scan and take a photo, or choose one from your library.

When another way works better

  • Packaged food: the label is more exact than a photo. Search for the product or enter the values from the label.
  • Drinks and soups: a photo can't show how much is in the cup or bowl. Search for it and choose the amount.
  • Foods you eat often: add them again from your favorites or recent foods.

Questions

How accurate is counting calories from a photo?

It's an estimate. Recognizing the food is usually the easier part; judging the portion from one photo is harder, and hidden fats and sugars can't be seen at all. Checking each item and adding what's hidden brings your log closer to what you ate.

Do I need to weigh my food?

No. A kitchen scale gives the most exact portions, but a checked photo estimate is quick enough to use at every meal. Weighing a food now and then helps you learn what a portion looks like.

Is a photo estimate medical advice?

No. Nutrition values from a photo are estimates for general information. Ringolo is not a medical device. If you follow a diet for medical reasons, talk to a doctor or dietitian.
